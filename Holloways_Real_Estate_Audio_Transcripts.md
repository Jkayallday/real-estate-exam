### Track 01 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (1:40)
- **Audio File**: `audio/Track01.mp3`
- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track01.mp3`

> Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate Law. The state department that licenses and regulates real estate agents is the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs. We'll refer to this part of our state government as simply the Department, or LARA, which is an acronym for Licensing and Regulatory Affairs. That's the way it's done on the exam, and that's the way we'll do it within this audio program.
>
> One of the Department's jobs is to enforce Michigan's main license law, called the Michigan Occupational Code. Passed in 1980 as Public Act 299, the Occupational Code consolidated many of Michigan's occupational license laws. It repealed many licensing laws and wrapped all the rules for practicing certain occupations up into one big law to make it easier to enforce. Another of the Department's responsibilities is the licensing exam. They give and maintain the license exam.
>
> Now, the Michigan Occupational Code sets the rules for application requirements, lists actions considered violations, and provides for different penalties. The law also created boards to work very closely with the Department. There's a board for each occupation. The Cosmetology Board deals with cosmetology licensees, the Board of Real Estate Brokers and Salespersons deals with real estate licensees, and so on. Each of these boards is made up of nine people, six of which are licensed and three unlicensed members who represent the general public. The Board assists the Department in interpreting the licensing law. The Board reviews petitions from applicants who have been denied a license, and they also aid the Department in investigations of possible license violations.

### Track 02 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (2:30)
- **Audio File**: `audio/Track02.mp3`
- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track02.mp3`

> Michigan has three types of real estate license: broker's license, associate broker's license, and salesperson's license. Only a standard broker's license can be issued to a non-living entity like a corporation or an LLC. Broker's licenses, associate broker's licenses, can only be issued to people, human beings.
>
> Now, the Occupational Code, our main license law, defines a broker as someone who does any of the following acts for another person and for compensation regarding real estate: sell or offer to sell, buy or offer to buy, listing, leasing, negotiating a mortgage of real estate, negotiation for the construction of a building, and negotiating for the sale of a business or business opportunity that includes real property in the transaction. If you want to do any of these things for somebody else and for a fee, you have to have a broker's license.
>
> An associate broker is someone who has an associate broker's license and is either principal or non-principal. An individual who has an associate broker's license and also has an ownership interest or a position of authority in the company is said to be a principal associate broker. If someone has an associate broker's license and does not have an ownership interest or a position of authority in the company, then they're considered non-principal associate brokers.
>
> A salesperson is someone who has a salesperson's license and works as an agent for the brokerage. They can do anything that a broker or associate broker can do, only with permission of the broker.
>
> There are a few exceptions to the licensing requirements, such as a person who is selling their own property. An owner can sell up to five of their own properties in any 12-month period. Also exempt is someone who is selling under a court order, an attorney-in-fact, an attorney-at-law. Also exempt are auctioneers, and licensed or registered mortgage brokers, mortgage lenders, or mortgage servicers.
>
> Applicants for a salesperson's license must take an approved 40-hour real estate class, pass the state licensing examination, pay the license fee, and electronically be accepted by their broker. Applicants for a broker or associate broker's license must have taken 90 hours of approved real estate classes, had three years of full-time real estate experience or its equivalent, and pass the state licensing examination, pay the license fee, and file a license application.

### Track 03 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (4:03)
- **Audio File**: `audio/Track03.mp3`
- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track03.mp3`

> Each licensee receives a pocket card which must be kept with them when working in real estate. The Department has the power to issue, deny, suspend, revoke, or place a limitation on a license. They also have the power to assess fines not to exceed $10,000, censure, or place a license on probation, and require a licensee to post a bond or cash deposit not to exceed $5,000.
>
> Some of the practices which are illegal and subject to the penalties are fraud, lack of good moral character, gross negligence, false advertising, or incompetence. It's also illegal to act for more than one party in the same transaction without the knowledge and consent of all parties involved in writing.
>
> Licensees must account for and remit any money that they have collected which belongs to others. A salesperson that collects a deposit must give the deposit to his or her employing broker immediately. The broker must then place the deposit into the company's trust account within two banking days after the offer is accepted by all parties involved. If the offer was signed by the seller on Monday afternoon, for example, the broker would only have through Wednesday to deposit those funds—two banking days. A licensee can also act as a courier of that deposit directly to a closing entity. If the brokerage chooses to do this, they do not have to keep a trust account.
>
> Brokers must keep a record, a journal, and a ledger to account for escrow funds. The journal lists each deposit and each withdrawal from the trust account and has a running balance which shows how much money is in that account at all times. Additionally, there's a ledger card or file for each transaction which shows the moneys being held for that specific buyer or seller, or landlord or tenant. A broker may keep up to $2,000 of their own money in their trust account to keep the account open when funds grow low.
>
> The Department may place a limitation on a license. The Department can restrict the function, a specific time, or geographic area of a licensee. Additionally, they can require that restitution be made, and regular financial statements, proof of competence, or again a bond or cash deposit not to exceed $5,000, and/or regular reports be submitted to the Department. The licensee could also be required to have all of his or her contracts reviewed by an attorney, and/or the licensee can be required to place escrow funds in an account which can only be disbursed under certain conditions.
>
> A broker can only do business and advertise under the name that appears on the license itself, exactly as it appears on the license itself. So a broker who wishes to use an assumed name, such as Roger's Realty or Holloway's Realty, would need to have that broker's license issued in that name. To do this, you'd send a copy of the certificate of assumed name—we call it a DBA here in Michigan—certified by the county clerk in the county where the certificate is on file, or if you're a corporation or an LLC and like to do business under its name, you just send whatever documents are used to create that LLC. For a corporation, it would be the Articles of Incorporation, for example. You send that in to the Department and they'll issue your broker's license in the name of that LLC, corporation, or DBA form.
>
> All licensees must take 18 hours of continuing education to renew their three-year license, two hours of which must be core information covering topics relevant to changes in economic conditions, laws, rules, and court cases regarding real estate. This part is referred to as the law update. You must take a law update every year. Annual continuing education requirements must be completed by October 31st of each year. If a licensee is at least 60 days late on meeting the licensing requirements, the Department can fine them up to $3,000 and require an additional eight hours of continuing education as punishment. That's harsh.

### Track 04 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (3:52)
- **Audio File**: `audio/Track04.mp3`
- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track04.mp3`

> If a licensee allowed their license to lapse, they may get relicensed within three years by simply taking the continuing education classes they missed and requesting relicensure at www.michigan.gov/icola. iCOLA stands for Internet Commercial and Occupational Licensing Application. After the three-year period, the applicant must request a relicensure, pay the license fee, and meet one of the following requirements: complete six hours of continuing education for each of the years the license was lapsed, or retake the 40-hour fundamentals course, or retake the salesperson's exam.
>
> Salespeople can transfer from one broker to another by filing a transfer form and paying a fee. Associate brokers are either principals of the company—principal associate brokers—or non-principal associate brokers. Non-principal associate brokers can transfer from one company to another by using a similar method to the salesperson's transfer; instead of paying a transfer fee, however, they pay a new license fee. Principal associate brokers cannot transfer. One reason is you're allowed to have as many principal associate broker's licenses with different companies as you want, so you don't need to transfer it, you just get a new one.
>
> Salespeople who wish to quit working for their broker must send that broker a written notice. The broker then has five days in which to mail the salesperson's license to the Department by certified mail. On the other side of that coin, a broker can fire a salesperson by sending a written notice to the salesperson's last known address and then send a copy of that notice to the Department by certified mail within five days.
>
> The Department will investigate a licensee's behavior if they receive a complaint or on its own motion. And informal conferences are set up to resolve complaints against licensees by trying to simply reach an agreement between the person filing the complaint and the person to which the complaint is against. Often a board member will attend. If an agreement is reached, the Board meets to determine whether or not to accept it. A board member who attended the conference is not allowed to vote on the decision. Now, some licensees waive the right to an informal conference and go right to a formal hearing. If the Board decides not to accept the agreement reached in an informal conference, also then the case goes to a formal hearing. An administrative law judge hears the case and determines if a violation has been committed. They send a report to the Board, and the Board reviews the case and sets the penalty. Remember, the Board always sets the penalty unless it's jail time, and only a judge can put you in jail. Now, even one violation of the licensing act could result in any of the penalties previously listed.
>
> If a broker's license is suspended or revoked, he or she must send his or her license and pocket card to the Department along with all of his salesperson's and branch office licenses as well. The Department wants all the paper bits back. All the salesperson's licenses are automatically suspended until they find a new broker to work for.
>
> Every Michigan broker must have an office within the state of Michigan. Brokers may have as many branch offices as desired, but each must have a branch office license. If a branch office is more than 25 miles from the city limits of the main office, it must have its own associate broker to directly manage it.
>
> A licensed real estate salesperson cannot collect compensation for listing, selling, leasing, etc., from anyone other than his or her employing broker. Licensees must have prior written consent from their client if they are to receive compensation in addition to their regular commission, such as for using a title insurance company or an abstract company, a mortgage company, or a home warranty company.

### Track 05 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (3:07)

- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track05.mp3`

> All advertising must include the broker's name as licensed. An ad which does not clearly indicate that the real estate company is involved is a blind ad and is illegal. All advertising done by a salesperson must be under his or her broker's supervision and in the name of that broker as licensed. Advertising by a team or an individual licensee must make the name of the brokerage equal to or greater in size than that of the team or the licensee. Property advertised for sale which is owned by a salesperson which is not his or her principal residence must be advertised in a broker's name. All property owned by a broker or associate broker advertised for sale or lease must clearly indicate that the property is being offered by a broker or associate broker; normally it just simply says broker-owned.
>
> If a licensee would like to buy real estate, he or she must disclose the fact to the seller that he or she is licensed, and also disclose if he or she is receiving any of the commission. This must be done as an individual disclosure before the purchase agreement is signed. A salesperson can only sell their principal residence as a For Sale By Owner. Any other property that they want to sell—a cabin up north, for example—must go through a broker; it must be listed. If a licensee is selling property in which he or she holds an interest as part owner, option, etc., this must be disclosed to any buyer in advance of the purchase agreement.
>
> The seller must receive a true copy of the signed listing agreement. Listings must have a definite expiration date and a non-discrimination clause, and net listings are illegal. Buyers must get a true copy of the signed purchase agreement. The licensee then presents the offer to the seller and obtains the seller's signature if they accept it. The buyer then gets a second copy after the sellers have signed. As stated in class, he signs, she signs, and he signs again, and then and only then is it a valid purchase agreement. There's a last copy that's given to the broker.
>
> Licensees must recommend to the buyer that a buyer receive a title insurance policy from the seller when the buyer signs the purchase agreement; it's a statutory recommendation.
>
> Salespeople cannot accept checks made out to them personally for escrow funds. Any deposits received by a salesperson must be given to his or her broker immediately or as soon as possible. The broker must deposit these funds again within two banking days in a non-interest-bearing demand account. A broker or associate broker must sign checks drawn from the trust account. Co-signatories may be used; however, the signature of a broker or associate broker must accompany the signature. If the brokerage keeps a trust account, then they must keep a journal and a ledger to keep track of deposits and withdrawals. Again, the journal keeps a chronological record of the deposits and withdrawals and keeps a running balance; the ledger keeps track of the deposits and withdrawals as they affect each transaction or listing.

### Track 06 — Lesson 1, Michigan Real Estate law (1:38)
- **Audio File**: `audio/Track06.mp3`
- **Source URL**: `https://hollowaysinstitute.biz/wp-content/uploads/Track06.mp3`

> This finally ends the Occupational Code. Now on to other Michigan laws relating to real estate which may come up on your state exam.
>
> The Truth in Renting Act forbids clauses in residential leases that would lessen the tenant's rights. It requires that the lease agreement contain the name and address of the landlord and requires the following disclosure: "NOTICE: Michigan law establishes rights and obligations to rental agreements. This agreement is required to comply with the Truth in Renting Act. If you have a question about the interpretation or legality of a provision of this agreement, you may want to seek assistance from an attorney or other qualified person."
>
> The Landlord-Tenant Relationship Act addresses the relationship between the landlord and the tenant as it concerns the security deposit. It might be called the Security Deposit Act on the exam. This law requires the use of commencement and termination inventory checklists. The security deposit is limited to one and one-half times a month's rent. It includes any required prepayment of rent other than rent for the first rental period. And the security deposit can only be used for the following: to reimburse the landlord for actual damages to the rental unit which are the direct result of conduct not considered normal habitation—so it does not include wear and tear—back rent, rent due for premature termination of the lease agreement by the tenant, and the tenant's unpaid utility bills. Those are the four things to which a landlord can keep the security deposit. The most a tenant can receive if the landlord violates the law is double the security deposit retained.

> Here's some important terminology.

>
> **Tort**: Tort is a civil wrong for which the law allows an injured party to recover damages from a wrongdoer.
>

In essence, assignment is transferring of all of someone's interest or obligation in a contract to somebody else. They're out of it. > Real estate contracts. The listing agreement is a contract between the broker and the seller. In a listing agreement, the seller agrees to pay the broker a commission if the broker can find or produce a ready, willing, and able purchaser to buy the property at the seller's price and terms. The amount of the commission is agreed upon by the broker and the seller; there's no entity that sets the commission.
>
> There are three different types of listing.
>
> **The open listing**: The seller can list with as many brokers as they want. Each of these brokers has the opportunity to find a buyer and earn a commission. At the same time, the seller can find the buyer and not pay a commission—in other words, sell the property as a For Sale By Owner.
>
> Next is **the exclusive or exclusive agency listing**: The seller agrees to list with only one broker, and if that broker finds a buyer, then they get a commission. But the seller still retains the right to find the buyer themselves and not pay a commission.
>
> The third and most common listing is **the exclusive right to sell listing**—the one with the longest name is the most common. With this listing, no matter who finds the buyer, the listing broker gets paid a commission. This is the one used by most brokers because it offers them the most protection.
>
> The next real estate contract is a **purchase agreement**. The purchase agreement is between the buyer and the seller. The real estate agent is not a party to this agreement; the agent just helps their client to negotiate the best deal possible. In this agreement, the buyer agrees to purchase the property at a specified price and at specified terms. They usually give the real estate agent a deposit to show their good faith. The real estate agent gives the deposit to the broker, who holds the deposit in a trust account or transfers it to the closing entity. The agent takes the offer to the seller, and the seller can either accept or reject the offer. The seller can reject the offer and make a counteroffer. If the seller agrees to sell the property to the purchasers at the specified price and terms, the seller signs the sales agreement, and the sales agent notifies the buyer of the seller's acceptance. This contract is now a binding agreement. As we said in class, he signs, and then she signs, and then the last copy of the purchase agreement goes to the broker.
>
> Two other real estate contracts are the **option** and **right of first refusal**. These are similar to one another, but there's one big difference: the option has set price and set terms, and the right of first refusal does not. An option is an agreement in which the optionor, the seller, gives an optionee, the buyer, the right to purchase the property at set price, set terms, and within a certain period of time. The option restricts the optionor, while on the other hand, the optionee is under no obligation to purchase the property; they simply have the option to do so. A right of first refusal is a right to buy the property if the seller decides to sell it in the future. If they decide to sell the property in the future, they have to offer it to the person with the right of first refusal first; they have the right to refuse to buy it first.
>
> The seller needs to give the purchaser evidence that the seller is indeed the owner of the property. Either an **abstract** or a **title insurance policy** is used. An abstract is a description of all of the recorded documents that affect that specific piece of property and shows the recorded chain of title. An abstract posted to date will show who is the current recorded title holder and also shows any recorded liens, easements, etc. Abstracts are not used very much anymore. The more common method of proving ownership is to purchase a title insurance policy for the buyers. The title insurance company will review the recorded chain of title, and if it looks sound, the company will insure that there is good title. If they're wrong, and if the title defect was one that was covered by the policy, the title insurance company will try to correct the defect or pay the policyholders the face amount of the policy. This is usually the purchase price.

### Track 28 — Lesson 4, Rights and Interests in Real Estate (2:34)

> Lesson 4, Rights and Interests in Real Estate. Any interest in land is called an **estate**, and there are two different types of estates. A **freehold estate** is an actual ownership of the property. A **leasehold estate** is not ownership of real property; it's some other type of interest that one might have in property, such as a lease.
>
> There are three main freehold, or ownership, interests that you can have in land: fee simple, qualified fee, and life estates. One of the main characteristics of ownership is your right to sell it. If you own land, you can sell it. Let's take a look at each of these forms of ownership.
>
> **Fee simple** is the highest ownership estate known. It's sometimes referred to simply as fee, or fee simple absolute. The holder is entitled to all the rights available in the property possible by law, the ownership is complete, and there's no time limit on its existence. Upon the death of the owner, the property usually descends to his or her heirs or survivors.
>
> The next kind of ownership is **qualified fee**. Determinable, conditional, or defeasible fees are estates that are similar to fee simple because they're ownership, except that the estate will terminate if a specific event occurs or doesn't occur. For example, Mary granted her 20 acres to the church with the condition that the property only be used as a church retreat. The deed stated that if the property ever ceased to be used for that purpose, the ownership would revert back to Mary.
>
> A very strange form of ownership is **life estates**. A life estate is ownership of the property, but only during the lifetime of someone we call the life tenant, or the lifetime of another. The owner of the life estate—the life tenant, they call them—is not allowed to commit **waste**. Waste would occur through a failure to pay property taxes, insurance, or mortgage payments. Any act on the land that substantially impairs the security value of the real estate is considered waste. Or another way to look at it would be any action on the property that would keep the remainderman from getting it after the life tenant dies. Example: John gave his mother a life estate in the house that he owned. Upon the death of John's mother, the ownership of the house would revert back to John. John is the remainderman, the reversioner.
>
> Because fee simple is the highest and most common form of ownership, it breaks down into four subcategories: severalty, common, joint, and entirety.

> **Severalty**—sever all ties. This is ownership by one person only. If you break up the word severalty down into its syllables, it's sever-al-ty, sever all ties. That's what this person has done: severed all ties with everybody else and they own property all by themselves—severalty. Upon the death of a tenant in severalty, the property descends to his or her heirs or devisees.

> **Tenants in common**: Ownership by two or more persons. Upon the death of a tenant in common, his or her interest in the property descends to the deceased's heirs or devisees. You are allowed to have dissimilar proportions of ownership; one person can own 10%, another person can own 14%, and so on. Tenants in common requires the **unity of possession**, and here's what that means: Each owner owns an undivided fractional interest in the property. You'd own a third interest or an eighth interest, it doesn't matter; the physical property, however, is not divided up. You would own your proportional share of every doorknob, every fiber in the carpet, for example.
>
> Now, **joint tenancy**: Ownership by two or more persons, but includes what's called the **right of survivorship**. Upon the death of a joint tenant, his or her share of the property is divided among the surviving owners only. If one of the owners passes away, their interest does not descend to their heirs or devisees; you can't will your interest to somebody else. It automatically increases the value of the surviving joint owners. Also requires the unities of possession, interest, time, and title.

> - The **unity of interest** means each owner must own exactly the same percentage of the property; if there are three owners, each must own a third, and so on.
> - The **unity of time** means each owner must take possession at the same time.
> - The **unity of title**: Each owner must own exactly in the same way; two people can't own in common and two others own in joint.
>
> **Tenants by the entirety** is set up between husband and wife. This is a specific type of joint tenancy. Upon the death of either spouse, the property will belong to the surviving spouse. It requires the unities of possession, interest, time, and title just like joint tenancy, but it also includes something called the **unity of person**. The marital unit is looked upon as being one single entity. Each is said to own 100%.

> A **license** is a personal privilege to use somebody else's property for a specific purpose. Because it's a privilege, it's revocable.
